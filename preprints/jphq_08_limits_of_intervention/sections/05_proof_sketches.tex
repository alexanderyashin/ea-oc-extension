\section{Proof Sketches}

We provide proof sketches that make explicit which \emph{structural features} of
the OC/ETS framework are responsible for each result.
No additional assumptions beyond threshold-defined admissibility, boundary
structure, and phase terminality are used.
The arguments are geometric and topological in nature, not dynamical or
optimization-based.

\subsection{Sketch for Theorem~\ref{thm:saturation}}

The admissible set $\Omega$ is defined by inequality constraints
$\ThetaSys(\omega)\le 0$.
At points near $\partial\Omega$, at least one component of $\ThetaSys$ is
\emph{active}, meaning that the local cone of admissible directions in the
ambient space collapses relative to the interior.

Any intervention $\mathcal{I}$ that attempts to enact a genuine
boundary-crossing change must therefore either:
(i) move in directions that violate at least one active constraint, breaking
$\ThetaSys$-preservation, or
(ii) avoid acting on those constraint-tight states altogether.
The region $\Omega^\ast$ consists precisely of states where admissible
directions are so restricted that no nontrivial boundary-crossing mapping can be
defined while preserving all thresholds.

\subsection{Sketch for Theorem~\ref{thm:no_free}}

The admissibility boundary $\partial\Omega$ is the locus where feasible degrees
of freedom shrink due to active constraints.
A $\ThetaSys$-preserving intervention acting across the boundary cannot move
freely in state space; it must route changes along the remaining admissible
directions.

This routing enforces one of two structural consequences:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Continuity loss.}
Constraint-compatible routing typically compresses trajectories or disconnects
previously connected subsets of $\Omega$, reducing the measure of structural
continuity $k$ for at least some states.
\item \emph{Phase transition.}
Alternatively, maintaining threshold satisfaction may require suppressing
cycles or flows that are necessary for closure in the OC sense.
When such closure conditions fail, the system necessarily crosses into a
different phase (e.g.\ $\INERTIA$, $\COLLAPSE$, or $\STOP$).
\end{enumerate}

Hence, a boundary-crossing intervention that preserves all thresholds cannot
simultaneously guarantee non-decreasing continuity and phase preservation on a
nontrivial domain.

\subsection{Sketch for Proposition~\ref{prop:trivial}}

If an intervention never confronts $\partial\Omega$, then by definition it
operates entirely within the interior of the admissible set.
Such operators can only re-parameterize existing degrees of freedom and cannot
realize changes whose feasibility is blocked precisely at the admissibility
boundary.
Therefore, safety (threshold preservation, non-decreasing $k$, and phase
preservation) is achieved at the cost of structural reach.

\subsection{Sketch for Theorem~\ref{thm:stop}}

In the OC/ETS framework, the phase $\STOP$ is defined as terminal: it corresponds
to the loss of admissible continuation due to threshold death, collapse of
closure, or exhaustion of feasible cycles.
Any $\ThetaSys$-preserving intervention sequence would require an admissible
path back to $\OK$.
Terminality excludes the existence of such a path by definition of the phase
partition, rendering $\STOP$ irreversible under OC/ETS-consistent operations.
