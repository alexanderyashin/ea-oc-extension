\section{Boundary and Phase Effects}

\subsection{Why the boundary is the hard region}

Near the admissible boundary $\partial\Omega$, the feasible set is shaped by
inequality constraints and is generically non-smooth. Two consequences matter:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Directional collapse:} the set of admissible directions shrinks as
constraints tighten, making many ``intended'' interventions infeasible without
threshold violation.
\item \textbf{Nonlocal propagation:} enforcing one threshold often shifts other
potentials or flows (OC coupling), so local changes near the boundary can induce
global changes in $J$ and $C$, and therefore in $k$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase transitions as constraint-induced bifurcations}

Even when an operator is $\Theta$-preserving pointwise, it may alter cycles $C$
and flows $J$ required for maintaining a phase.
As a result, phase transitions are not necessarily controllable by ``making the
operator smaller'': the transition is triggered by the structural fact that some
closure conditions cannot be maintained under tightened constraints.

\begin{remark}
This preprint does not claim that intervention is ``hopeless''.
It claims that the combination of (i) boundary-crossing intent and (ii) strict
threshold preservation imposes unavoidable tradeoffs: either continuity is
degraded somewhere, or the system changes phase somewhere.
\end{remark}
