\subsection{Positioning with Respect to Related Work}

\paragraph{Context and motivation.}
A broad range of literatures address enterprises and complex socio-technical
systems, including systems theory, control and optimization, enterprise
architecture (EA) and information systems (IS), and managerial diagnostics.
Despite shared terminology (state, structure, observation, performance), these
traditions are typically organized around objectives that differ fundamentally
from the problem treated here.

This paper focuses on \emph{structural diagnosability} under incomplete
observability: whether the available observation surface permits a principled
distinction between structurally different enterprise states, or whether such
a distinction is formally impossible. The contribution is diagnostic rather
than prescriptive, and negative results (non-identifiability, undecidability,
STOP) are treated as informative endpoints rather than failures.

\paragraph{Systems theory and observability.}
Classical systems theory studies observability and identifiability for systems
whose model class is fixed or parameterized a priori. The central question is
whether internal states or parameters can be reconstructed from outputs under
known inputs and dynamics.

In contrast, the enterprise setting addressed here does not assume a single
fixed model class. Feasible structure is itself constrained by organizational,
technical, legal, and economic boundaries, and these constraints may evolve
with the state of the system. Observation channels are heterogeneous, partial,
and often endogenous. As a result, the relevant diagnostic question is not
whether a state can be reconstructed under a known model, but whether current
evidence allows one to distinguish among \emph{structurally incompatible}
enterprise states at all. This shifts the focus from reconstruction to
diagnostic decidability.

\paragraph{Model-based diagnosis and fault isolation.}
Engineering approaches to model-based diagnosis and fault isolation provide
formal tools for detecting inconsistencies between observations and a given
model, and for isolating fault hypotheses. These approaches rely on a well-
defined fault taxonomy, a stable observation interface, and a separation
between system structure and control logic.

Enterprises violate these assumptions in a systematic way. Structural
incompatibilities are often global rather than local, fault classes are not
exhaustively enumerable, and feasibility constraints cannot be factored out of
the diagnostic problem. The present work adopts the diagnostic attitude of
explicit hypothesis spaces and negative results, but without assuming that a
complete fault taxonomy or a uniquely identifiable structure exists.

\paragraph{Control theory and optimization.}
Control and optimization frameworks presuppose a controllable interface, an
objective function (often expressed via KPIs or utility), and a stable mapping
from interventions to outcomes. These assumptions enable performance-oriented
reasoning, but they implicitly require that the system state is sufficiently
well-defined to support optimization.

Structural diagnosability precedes any such reasoning. If multiple incompatible
enterprise states are consistent with the same observation surface, optimizing
against any single assumed state risks producing spurious certainty. For this
reason, the paper does not propose control policies or optimization schemes.
Instead, it isolates the precondition for rational intervention: that the
enterprise state is diagnostically well-posed under available evidence. Where
this precondition fails, the correct outcome is a negative diagnostic result.

\paragraph{EA/IS frameworks and architectural practice.}
EA and IS frameworks provide structured representations of enterprises through
layers, viewpoints, and governance processes. Their primary function is to
support coordination, communication, and planning. Architectural descriptions
are often treated as authoritative abstractions of the underlying enterprise.

The diagnostic limitation is that enterprises typically maintain multiple
concurrent representations (declared, current, feasible), and the mapping from
observations to structural claims is neither injective nor stable. EA practice
often resolves this through process and social validation. In contrast, this
paper treats the problem as one of formal diagnosability: whether the available
observation surface suffices, in principle, to justify a structural claim,
independent of governance or consensus mechanisms.

\paragraph{KPI-driven evaluation and managerial case literature.}
KPI-driven approaches evaluate enterprises through selected indicators, while
managerial case literature emphasizes narrative coherence and retrospective
causal explanation. Both can support action under uncertainty, but neither
addresses the epistemic question of whether a structural diagnosis is sound.

Indicators and narratives may remain actionable even when the underlying state
is not uniquely identifiable. Structural diagnosability, by contrast, requires
explicit acknowledgment of non-uniqueness: that the same observations may be
compatible with mutually exclusive structural interpretations. This paper
therefore avoids KPI logic and case-based prescriptions, and treats
non-identifiability as an explicit diagnostic outcome.

\paragraph{What this paper does \emph{not} attempt to do.}
To avoid scope confusion, we state explicitly what is out of scope:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It does not propose an EA method, maturity model, or governance process.
  \item It does not define KPIs, targets, or performance objectives.
  \item It does not provide optimization algorithms or intervention policies.
  \item It does not assume that enterprises can be made fully observable.
  \item It does not claim superiority through improved outcomes or efficiency.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Summary.}
Relative to adjacent literatures, the distinctive stance of this paper is to
treat structural diagnosability under incomplete observability as the primary
object of analysis. Negative results are not treated as deficiencies, but as
formal boundaries on what can be inferred. In this sense, the contribution is
diagnostic-first: it establishes when enterprise states are well-defined
objects of inference, and when they are not.
