\section{Implications (Epistemic Only)}
\label{sec:implications}

In this paper, ``implications'' are \emph{epistemic consequences} of the formal
setting: they restrict what can be inferred (or claimed) from a given
observational interface and a fixed vocabulary boundary
(\texttt{ETS.K\_EA.v1.1}). They are not operational recommendations.

\subsection{Implications for Inference Under Incomplete Observability}
\label{subsec:imp-incomplete-observability}

The formal results imply the following limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Non-uniqueness is the default.}
  Absent explicit identifiability conditions, the inverse mapping from
  observations to internal structure is set-valued: multiple incompatible
  internal states may be observationally indistinguishable.

  \item \textbf{Apparent stability may be epistemic, not structural.}
  A stable pattern of observations does not imply a stable internal structure.
  It may reflect observation coarseness, masking, or information loss.

  \item \textbf{``More data'' is not guaranteed to help.}
  Additional observations can be irrelevant if they do not reduce the
  indistinguishability classes induced by the interface. Therefore, claims of
  improved diagnosability require formal justification, not intuition.

  \item \textbf{Boundary effects dominate near critical regions.}
  Close to phase boundaries (including STOP), small changes in observation or
  assumptions can cause discontinuous changes in admissible inferences. This is
  an epistemic instability, not necessarily an operational one.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implications for the Meaning of Phase Regions}
\label{subsec:imp-phase-regions}

A phase-region label produced by the predicates in this paper must be read as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A classification within the formal semantics,} conditional on
  the stated assumptions and on the declared observation interface.

  \item \textbf{Not a statement about managerial desirability.}
  A region is neither ``good'' nor ``bad'' in an operational sense; it is only
  a statement about diagnosability/existence conditions in the model.

  \item \textbf{Not a promise of recoverability.}
  The formalism does not assert that systems in non-STOP regions are recoverable,
  controllable, or improvable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implications of STOP}
\label{subsec:imp-stop}

STOP has three epistemic implications:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Inference collapse:} within the present interface and
  assumptions, non-degenerate diagnosis is not justified.

  \item \textbf{Constraint violation or undecidability:} the STOP predicate
  indicates either violation of existence/consistency constraints or loss of
  diagnosability required for meaningful state claims.

  \item \textbf{Non-substitutability:} STOP cannot be replaced by a positive
  diagnosis without adding external assumptions, new primitives, or additional
  semantics beyond the allowed vocabulary.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implications for Claims and Reporting}
\label{subsec:imp-claims-reporting}

The results impose negative constraints on what a scientifically valid report may
claim:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No causal claims without identifiability.}
  If multiple internal states are compatible with the same observations, causal
  statements about internal structure are underdetermined.

  \item \textbf{No operational conclusions from epistemic regions.}
  Mapping an epistemic classification to a decision recommendation is an
  additional step requiring a policy model, which is out of scope here.

  \item \textbf{No universality without a declared mapping.}
  Any application to a concrete enterprise must explicitly declare the mapping
  from enterprise artifacts/observations to the formal objects; otherwise, the
  result is not meaningfully interpretable.
\end{enumerate}
