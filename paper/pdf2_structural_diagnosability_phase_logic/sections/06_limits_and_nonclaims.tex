\section{Limits and Non-Claims}
\label{sec:limits-nonclaims}

This document presents a \emph{diagnostic} formalization of
(i) structural diagnosability conditions and
(ii) phase-logic predicates, including STOP,
under the assumptions and symbol vocabulary fixed by the formal setting and by
\texttt{ETS.K\_EA.v1.1}.
It does not provide prescriptions, control rules, optimization objectives, or
decision guidance.

\subsection{Non-Claims (Explicit)}
\label{subsec:nonclaims-explicit}

The following statements are explicitly \emph{not} asserted anywhere in this
paper:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No control or optimization claim.}
  The paper does not claim the ability to control, optimize, stabilize, or
  improve an enterprise system, nor to select actions that move the system within
  or between phase regions.

  \item \textbf{No algorithmic procedure claim.}
  The paper does not provide algorithms, procedures, or operational recipes for
  computing diagnoses or enforcing constraints in real organizations.

  \item \textbf{No KPI or managerial metric claim.}
  The paper does not define KPIs, performance targets, maturity models, or
  managerial scorecards.
  Any numerical illustration, if present, serves only as an internal
  demonstration of formal predicates.

  \item \textbf{No causal attribution from symptoms to structure.}
  The paper does not claim that observed incidents or measurements uniquely
  identify internal structure.
  In general, multiple distinct internal configurations may be compatible with
  the same observable manifestations.

  \item \textbf{No completeness claim under partial observability.}
  Under incomplete observability, the framework does not guarantee that every
  true internal failure mode is diagnosable from the available observations.

  \item \textbf{No universality beyond the formal setting.}
  The definitions are not claimed to be universally adequate for every
  organization without an explicit, justified mapping from that organization to
  the formal objects constrained by \texttt{ETS.K\_EA.v1.1}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why Control and Optimization Questions Are Ill-Posed Here}
\label{subsec:why-control-illposed}

A recurrent reviewer misreading is to treat any phase-space representation as
implicitly actionable.
Within the present formalism, such an inference is invalid for structural
reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic statements are not policies.}
  A diagnostic statement has the form:
  ``given assumptions $A$ and observations $O$, the set of compatible internal
  realizations is $\mathcal{K}_{\text{compat}}$''.
  A control or optimization statement requires additional objects: a policy or
  action space, a transition model under interventions, and a goal functional.
  None of these are introduced here, and introducing them would violate the
  diagnostic-only scope.

  \item \textbf{Underdetermination under partial observability.}
  When observations are incomplete, inverse inference is generally non-unique.
  Without uniqueness, optimization of interventions is not well-defined, since
  different compatible internal realizations may require incompatible actions.

  \item \textbf{Specification boundary (no new primitives).}
  \texttt{ETS.K\_EA.v1.1} fixes the admissible vocabulary and precludes the
  introduction of managerial primitives such as objectives, utilities, or ROI.
  Questions of the form ``what should be done?'' therefore lie outside the formal
  language of this paper.

  \item \textbf{Category distinction: epistemic versus operational.}
  The results constrain what can be \emph{known}, \emph{ruled out}, or
  \emph{left undecided} from a given observation interface.
  They do not specify what can be \emph{implemented} in a socio-technical
  organization.
\end{enumerate}

Accordingly, control and optimization questions are not merely unanswered; they
are \emph{ill-posed within the present formalism}.

\subsection{STOP as a Valid Scientific Outcome}
\label{subsec:stop-valid-outcome}

STOP is treated as a first-class outcome because it can be the correct diagnostic
conclusion under the formal semantics:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{STOP is an epistemic classification.}
  STOP indicates that, under the admitted predicates and available observations,
  the system lies in a region where diagnosability fails, existence or consistency
  constraints are violated, or the formal interface does not support
  non-degenerate inference.

  \item \textbf{STOP may be maximally informative.}
  In certain regimes, the statement ``no non-degenerate diagnosis is justified
  within this interface'' is strictly more informative than any positive but
  unsupported classification.

  \item \textbf{STOP is falsifiable at the interface level.}
  If an observational regime classified as STOP nevertheless permits stable and
  unique inference under the same formal assumptions, then the STOP predicate, or
  its prerequisites, is incorrect and must be revised \emph{within the admitted
  vocabulary}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Falsifiability Boundaries}
\label{subsec:falsifiability-boundaries}

The paper contains two classes of claims, each with a distinct falsifiability
mode:

\paragraph{(F1) Internal formal claims.}
Statements of the form ``given Definitions $X$ and Assumptions $Y$, Proposition
$Z$ holds'' are falsified by a \emph{formal counterexample}: an explicit
construction within the formal setting that satisfies $X$ and $Y$ while
violating $Z$.

\paragraph{(F2) Projection and interface claims.}
Statements relating formal objects to an empirical enterprise are conditional on
a declared observational interface.
They are falsified if at least one of the following can be demonstrated:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Specification violation:}
  the argument requires primitives not permitted by \texttt{ETS.K\_EA.v1.1}, or
  contradicts an explicit constraint of that specification.

  \item \textbf{Incoherent mapping:}
  the observational interface cannot be instantiated without altering the
  formal meaning of the symbols, i.e., the mapping is not semantics-preserving.

  \item \textbf{Interface insufficiency:}
  for a claimed diagnosability regime, the observation channel is provably
  insufficient, for example because information loss renders the inverse problem
  non-identifiable under the stated assumptions.

  \item \textbf{Indeterminate predicate evaluation:}
  under the same formal assumptions and the same declared interface, the
  diagnostic predicates cannot be evaluated unambiguously.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hard Limits of the Framework}
\label{subsec:hard-limits}

Even within its intended scope, the framework cannot guarantee:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Uniqueness of inferred internal structure} from observations,
  unless explicit identifiability conditions are met.

  \item \textbf{Completeness of diagnosis} under partial observability; some
  internal degradations may remain observationally indistinguishable.

  \item \textbf{Operational feasibility of interventions,}
  since interventions are not elements of the formal language employed here.

  \item \textbf{Empirical validity of a chosen mapping} from a real organization
  to the formal objects; such mappings require external evidence and validation
  beyond the scope of this paper.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Interpretive Takeaway}
\label{subsec:takeaway}

The correct reading is:

\begin{quote}
This paper specifies the conditions under which structural diagnosis is
well-posed in a formal sense, and the conditions under which the only honest
diagnostic outcome is STOP.
It does not prescribe actions, objectives, or optimization criteria.
\end{quote}
