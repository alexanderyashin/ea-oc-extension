\section{Problem Statement}

Enterprises are complex systems whose internal structure is only partially observable.
Architectural elements, dependencies, constraints, and tensions evolve in ways that are not directly accessible through external observation.
Instead, analysis typically relies on projections such as metrics, events, reports, or aggregated indicators.
These projections constitute observable manifestations of the enterprise, but they do not provide a one-to-one mapping to the underlying structural configuration.

This paper examines the problem of determining whether, and to what extent, an enterprise can be \emph{structurally diagnosed} based on such observable projections.
Structural diagnosability is understood here as an epistemic property: the ability to infer structural phase membership of an enterprise from available observations, given a fixed formal model.
The question is not whether a particular enterprise state is favorable or unfavorable, but whether it is distinguishable, in principle, from other structurally different states.

Formally, the enterprise is modeled as a continuum \(K_{EA}\) with an internal state space and a corresponding observable projection.
Multiple internal configurations may map to identical or near-identical observable outcomes.
When this occurs, state-level diagnosis becomes undecidable, even if observations are precise.
The paper therefore shifts the focus from exact state identification to phase-level diagnosability, where structurally equivalent configurations are grouped into equivalence classes.

The core problem addressed is the following:
given a set of observable manifestations, under what conditions can an enterprise be diagnosed as belonging to a specific structural phase, and under what conditions is such a diagnosis fundamentally underdetermined?

Several boundaries are explicit.
The paper does not propose methods for improving observability, refining measurements, or influencing enterprise behavior.
It does not introduce new managerial constructs, optimization criteria, or intervention operators.
It does not assume the availability of complete or noise-free data.
Instead, it treats incomplete observability as a structural condition and analyzes its implications for diagnostic claims.

Within these constraints, the objective is to clarify the logical status of diagnostic statements about enterprises.
A diagnosis may be possible, impossible, or falsifiable only at the level of structural phases.
Recognizing these limits is essential for distinguishing meaningful diagnostic knowledge from claims that exceed what the model and observations can support.
